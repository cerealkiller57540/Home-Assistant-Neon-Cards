Reclaiming a BTicino/Legrand EasyKit door station on the LAN, no cloud: recover the TLS client identity, beat the static-ECDH cipher wall (mbedTLS 3.6.x), register over SIP-TLS locally, get the ring and an on-ring photo into Home Assistant, and open the gate/lock/light with a local SIP MESSAGE. A write-up for owners of a BTicino / Legrand EasyKit door station who want it to work without the vendor cloud. It documents, step by step, how the door station was brought back onto the local network: doorbell ring, a photo of the visitor and gate opening in Home Assistant, all running locally in production. Read it if you own this kit, or if you are reverse-engineering a similar SIP-based door entry system.

Reclaiming a BTicino / Legrand EasyKit door station on the local network

Status: the hard parts are proven in production. The reverse-engineering chain below works end to end: the TLS client identity is recovered from the vendor app, the mutual-TLS handshake to the door station succeeds, and a SIP REGISTER is accepted (200 OK) — all on the LAN, with no cloud. On top of that, a background client catches the incoming call and grabs a photo of the visitor on ring (§9), and the gate/lock is opened locally over the same SIP channel (§8) — both running and proven, both cloud-free. What is left is polish (full live media, richer event history, night-time tuning); those parts are in §10.

What this does. This project reads the door station — it knows when someone rings and grabs a photo of the visitor on ring — and it drives it: the gate/lock-release command travels over the same local SIP channel, as a small SIP MESSAGE, with no cloud (see §8). Earlier write-ups (this one included, in an earlier revision) claimed opening had to go through the vendor cloud over HTTPS. Reading the decompiled app (§2) showed that is false: it is local, and it is proven.

What this repo is — read this before you judge reproducibility. This is a method with proofs, not a drop-in product. What ships here reproduces the hard, transferable parts: the toolchain build, the decompile, the Frida identity capture, the TLS handshake and the SIP REGISTER (scripts/). The always-on client that ties it together — catch the ring, write the AVI, pull the frame, publish over MQTT, emit the open MESSAGE (§8/§9) — runs in the author's house and is what "proven in production" refers to, but it is not published: it is welded to device-specific identifiers that must not leave the owner's vault (§7). So: the handshake and register are reproducible from this repo today; the ring/photo/open service you must assemble yourself from the documented shape in §8–§9. Publishing a cleanly anonymised version of that service is the main open task.

One load-bearing caveat, up front: the whole approach rides on mbedTLS 3.6.x, whose LTS support ends around March 2027, because the door only speaks static-ECDH cipher suites that mbedTLS 4.x and modern OpenSSL have removed. After that you are on an out-of-support TLS library or your own fork. This is structural, not a detail — see §4.

Why bother — planned obsolescence

The hardware is a competent little SIP/TLS intercom. It speaks a standard protocol (SIP over TLS), it has a camera, it can register internal handsets. But the vendor gates almost everything behind their app and their cloud, ships firmware that stops gaining features, and gives the owner no local, documented way in. The device is capable of far more than the editor lets you do with it, and the editor has no intention of changing that.

This is a small push back against that: the box is yours, it sits on your network, and the protocol it speaks is open. The only thing in the way is that the vendor encrypted the keys to their own device and handed you an app instead of a manual. This write-up is how you get the keys back and talk to your own hardware directly.

Nothing here is a vendor secret in the cryptographic sense. The method recovers your own device's identity, which already lives on your own phone — the app just keeps it encrypted at rest. Everything is done on hardware you own, on your own LAN.

⚠️ Read this before anything else — scope, legality, redaction

  • Do this only on a device you own, on your own network. Pulling a TLS client identity out of an app and using it to authenticate is fine when the identity is yours and the device is yours. It is not fine on someone else's building.
  • No real identifiers are published here. Everything device-specific is a placeholder: <doorstation-ip>, <sip-user>, <sip-domain>, <client-cn>, sip:<ext>. The actual TLS private key, client certificate and SIP password are not in this repo and never will be — they live in a password manager and are read on demand. See §7.
  • The whole chain is device-specific. Your cipher-suite list, your SIP domain, your account format will differ. The method transfers; the values do not.
  • "Against the app's terms" is not the same as "against the law". Extracting your own identity from the vendor app very likely breaches the app's terms of service. That is a contract question, not a criminal one, and in many places interoperability and the right to repair your own hardware are explicitly protected — but terms-of-service and the law are different things, your jurisdiction is yours to check, and nothing here is legal advice. This is written for the owner acting on their own device; don't read it as a licence to ignore local law.

0. Prerequisites — before you start

This is a serious afternoon (or two). It is "hard but doable", and the thing that makes it feel scarier than it is, is that it touches four worlds at once: Android reverse-engineering, a C build toolchain, TLS internals, and SIP. You do not need to be an expert in all four — but you need each tool present and working before you begin, or you will blame step 5 for a step-0 problem.

The reference environment: WSL2 (Ubuntu)

The write-up assumes a Windows 11 host with WSL2 running Ubuntu 22.04/24.04. That is the path this project actually used, and it is the most reproducible one for a Windows developer:

  • The Linux side (WSL) builds and runs everything C/Java: jadx, mbedTLS, PJSIP, pjsua, the background service. Building mbedTLS/PJSIP on Windows natively (MSYS2/mingw) is possible but a minefield — don't, for a first run.
  • The Windows side runs the Android emulator and talks to it: the Android SDK, adb and Frida all run fine on Windows, and the emulator is far happier on the Windows host than inside WSL. So: emulator + adb + Frida on Windows, build/run everything else on WSL. The one file you carry across (\\wsl$\… or a shared folder) is the recovered identity.

If you already run a native Linux box or VM, use it — build there and run the emulator there too; skip the WSL notes. The only reason to split across Windows and WSL is that the emulator and USB stack behave better on the Windows host.

The Android side — use an emulator, not a rooted phone

The identity extraction (§3) needs an Android where Frida can spawn and instrument the app. A rooted physical phone works but is rare and risky nowadays — so the recommended path is a throwaway Android Studio / SDK emulator (AVD), which gives you a clean, disposable, root-capable Android for free:

  • Create an AVD with a "Google APIs" system image (not "Google Play") — the Google-APIs images let you run adb root and drop a frida-server, which the locked Play images don't.
  • Install the companion app's APK into the AVD (adb install app.apk). Pull the APK from a phone that has it (adb shell pm path <pkg> → adb pull), or get it from the vendor's store listing.
  • Networking caveat: the pairing/provisioning flow may want to reach the real door station on your LAN. The emulator sits behind a NAT, so make sure it can route to <doorstation-ip> (on Windows the default emulator network usually can; if not, bridge it or run the AVD on the host network). This is the one place the emulator path needs a glance, not the rooted-phone drama.

Everything after the extraction is pure Linux — no phone or emulator involved.

Also needed: network reach to the door station on the LAN (its IP and the SIP-TLS port).

The skills it assumes

Honest about the level: you should be comfortable running commands in a Linux shell, building a C project from source (and reading a build error), and poking at an Android app with adb. You do not need to read obfuscated Java fluently, write TLS code, or know SIP — the hard findings are already distilled into this document; you are reproducing them, not rediscovering them.

The short version

A BTicino/Legrand EasyKit door station is, under the vendor skin, a SIP endpoint protected by mutual TLS. To talk to it locally you need to present a client certificate it trusts — and that certificate, its private key, and the SIP account parameters are all sitting inside the companion Android app, encrypted at rest.

The chain is:

  1. Decompile the app and read how it actually talks to the door — this is the backbone of the whole effort. A static read of the APK tells you the SIP address format, the media directions, the encryption it expects, and — later — how ringing and door-opening really work. Everything else is guided by what you find here — §2.
  2. Pull the TLS identity out of the app. The key + cert are encrypted at rest; a small runtime hook on the decrypt primitive captures the plaintext the app itself produces — one targeted brick, not the whole job — §3.
  3. Discover the door's TLS constraints — it only accepts a handful of static-ECDH cipher suites, which modern OpenSSL has removed — so you must build the client on mbedTLS 3.6.x — §4.
  4. Prove the handshake with a raw TLS client — §5.
  5. Register over SIP-TLS with PJSIP, working around a certificate-name mismatch — §6.

After that you have a registered local endpoint. From there, the static read in §2 is what turns registration into real features: catching the ring and grabbing a photo on ring (§9), and even driving the door locally (§8) — both proven, both cloud-free.

1. What you're up against

The door station (<doorstation-ip> on your LAN) listens for SIP over TLS. Two things make a naive connection fail:

  • Mutual TLS. The server doesn't just present a cert, it demands a client cert signed by a CA it trusts. No client cert → the handshake dies. The only place that client identity exists, outside the device, is the vendor app.
  • A deliberately narrow TLS profile. The server negotiates only a few old cipher suites built on static ECDH (fixed-key, prime256v1). That matters enormously for tooling — see §4.

So before any SIP packet is sent, you have to (a) extract the identity and (b) have a TLS stack that can even complete the handshake.

2. Decompiling the app — the backbone

Almost nothing in this project was guessed. Every decision — which SIP address to dial, whether to offer video, which encryption the door demands, how the doorbell actually reaches you, how the app opens the gate — came out of reading the decompiled app. If you do one thing before touching the network, do this.

The companion app is an Android APK wrapping the Linphone SIP stack. Decompile it to readable Java with jadx:

# jadx 1.5.x, with a JDK 17 on PATH
jadx --no-res --show-bad-code -j2 -d out/     com.<vendor>.<app>.apk   # Java call path
jadx --no-src                     -d out-res/ com.<vendor>.<app>.apk   # resources (res/raw/*.rc)

--no-res keeps the first pass fast and small, but it also skips the one resource you need — res/raw/linphonerc (below). The second pass (--no-src) pulls the resources on their own, into out-res/resources/res/raw/. scripts/10-decompile.sh runs both.

You get a few thousand .java files. The class names are obfuscated (a.java, b.java, C0616f.java…), but the Linphone SDK calls are not — and those calls are the map. What reading it tells you, and why each point matters downstream:

  • The real SIP address is assembled at runtime. The only literal in the DEX is the user part (here, diy); the domain is appended from the default proxy config — i.e. the door's own cloud FQDN — not the LAN IP. Every early attempt that dialled the device by IP was dialling the wrong address. You only learn the right form by reading the call-building code.
  • The call that shows the hallway asks for video RecvOnly, audio inactive, and carries the target's device address as a custom SDP attribute — not a SIP header. That single fact decides whether a command-line client can reproduce the outgoing "see the hall" call at all (it can't — it can't inject an arbitrary SDP attribute), and it is also what tells you the incoming ring carries video you can grab without any such attribute (see §9).
  • Door-opening is a local SIP MESSAGE, not a cloud call. The "slide to open" action builds a small JSON-RPC body and sends it over the same local SIP channel. This is the single most important correction to the folklore around these devices — see §8.
  • The factory config ships in res/raw. The embedded linphonerc reveals the SIP transport, whether the server certificate's name is verified, and the media-encryption policy. Read it before you fight the handshake — it saves you from blaming the wrong layer.

Keep the decompiled tree around. Throughout the rest of this write-up, the answer to "but how do you know that?" is almost always "it's in the decompiled call path."

3. Pulling the TLS identity out of the app

With the call path understood, you still need the client identity the door's mutual-TLS demands: an EC P-256 private key, the matching X.509 client certificate, the CA chain, and the SIP account parameters (username, domain/realm, password, registrar). The cert and key are stored encrypted at rest — the app wraps them with a key held in the Android Keystore — while the public cert and CA chain sit in the clear.

You do not need to reverse the key-derivation or the storage format. The app decrypts its own identity in memory when it connects, so the clean capture is a small runtime hook on the decrypt primitive — one targeted brick on top of the static read, not a second reverse-engineering effort.

On Android this funnels through javax.crypto.Cipher. Hook doFinal and only act when the cipher is in DECRYPT mode — otherwise you also dump everything the app encrypts, which is noise:

// frida -U -f com.<vendor>.<app> -l dump_identity.js   (spawn, not attach — see below)
Java.perform(function () {
  const Cipher = Java.use('javax.crypto.Cipher');
  const DECRYPT_MODE = 2;

  // Remember the mode each Cipher instance was initialised with.
  Cipher.init.overload('int', 'java.security.Key').implementation = function (mode, key) {
    this._mode = mode;
    return this.init(mode, key);
  };
  // (hook the other init overloads the same way — several exist)

  function dump(tag, out) {
    if (this._mode !== DECRYPT_MODE || out === null) return;
    const b = Java.array('byte', out);
    console.log('\n--- doFinal DECRYPT (' + tag + ', ' + b.length + ' bytes) ---');
    // Print as hex AND as text; identity blobs are often PEM/ASN.1 or JSON.
    console.log(hexdump(b, { length: Math.min(b.length, 4096), ansi: false }));
  }

  Cipher.doFinal.overload('[B').implementation = function (input) {
    const out = this.doFinal(input);
    dump.call(this, 'doFinal([B)', out);
    return out;
  };
  Cipher.doFinal.overload().implementation = function () {
    const out = this.doFinal();
    dump.call(this, 'doFinal()', out);
    return out;
  };
});

Why hook the decrypt and not read the file? The blob on disk is ciphertext; the key to decrypt it lives in the Keystore and is applied at runtime. Hooking the decrypt output skips all of that — you get the finished plaintext the app was about to use, regardless of how it was stored or keyed.

The catch — force a fresh decrypt. The app only decrypts the identity when it has a reason to: typically during provisioning/pairing, or the first connection after a fresh install. An app that already has everything cached and idle may never fire doFinal with the material you want — and an identity written under an old Keystore key can even throw BadPaddingException. The reliable way is to make the app provision again: clear its data (or reinstall), spawn it under the hook (frida -f …, spawn-gating — hooked before the first crypto call, not attached after), then walk the pairing/login flow. The identity is decrypted as part of coming online, written fresh under the current Keystore key, right in front of your hook.

This step is the single point of failure for the whole chain. Everything downstream — the handshake, the REGISTER, the ring, the open — depends on one assumption: that the app decrypts its identity through javax.crypto.Cipher.doFinal in a Java process a Frida hook can reach. That holds today. It stops holding the moment the app moves the crypto out of reach: a native (NDK) TLS/keystore implementation, hardware-backed StrongBox keys that never leave the secure element, Play Integrity / attestation that refuses to run on a rooted or emulated device, or plain obfuscation/anti-Frida. Any app update can introduce any of these, and then this capture breaks and the rest is moot. The TLS and SIP work below is stable — it's standards. The identity extraction is the fragile hinge, and it's worth knowing that before you build on it.

4. The TLS wall: static-ECDH, and why OpenSSL won't do

With the identity in hand, the next wall is the handshake itself. The door station accepts only a small set of cipher suites, and they are all built on static ECDH over prime256v1 — the fixed-key elliptic-curve key exchange, e.g. the ECDH-ECDSA-* / ECDH-RSA-* families (note: ECDH-*, not the ephemeral ECDHE-*).

That single fact decides your whole toolchain:

  • OpenSSL 3.x cannot do it. Static-ECDH suites were removed from OpenSSL years ago. Any client built on a current OpenSSL — which is almost everything, including stock openssl s_client, current cURL, most language TLS bindings — will offer only ephemeral suites, the server will find no common suite, and the handshake fails at "no shared cipher". This is the single biggest time-sink; it looks like an auth problem but it's a cipher problem.
  • You need mbedTLS, and specifically the 3.6.x LTS line. mbedTLS still implements static-ECDH suites. Use 3.6.x, not 4.x — the 4.x line dropped them too. Build mbedTLS 3.6.x yourself.

So the client side of this project is built on mbedTLS 3.6.x. Everything downstream (the SIP stack) has to be built against that mbedTLS, not the system OpenSSL.

Longevity — plan for this dependency being legacy. This isn't a passing version quirk, it's a dead end that the whole ecosystem is walking away from. Static ECDH is deprecated by RFC 9325, OpenSSL dropped it in 2016, and mbedTLS 4.0.0 (released 2025-10-15) removed the static-ECDH / ECDH-ECDSA / ECDH-RSA key exchanges for TLS 1.2 — keeping only ephemeral ECDHE. The 3.6 LTS line keeps them, but its support window runs only to about March 2027. After that, staying on the door means staying on an out-of-support mbedTLS, or maintaining your own fork that re-enables the suites. And the next LTS is no escape hatch: mbedTLS 4.1 LTS (released March 2026, supported to March 2029) carries the 4.x removal forward — it still does not offer static ECDH. So "just wait for the next LTS" does not get you a supported library that talks to this door; the gap only widens. The real fix isn't on our side — it's the door's firmware offering a modern key exchange, which the vendor has no incentive to ship. Know this going in: the cryptography that lets you reclaim the device is itself on borrowed time.

5. Proving the handshake

Before dragging a whole SIP stack into it, prove the TLS layer alone with mbedTLS's own sample client, ssl_client2, built from the 3.6.x tree.

# Build mbedTLS 3.6.x with the sample programs, then:
./programs/ssl/ssl_client2 \
    server_addr=<doorstation-ip> server_port=<sip-tls-port> \
    key_file=client.key crt_file=client.crt \
    debug_level=2

Reading the debug output:

  • You want to reach "Protocol is TLSvX.X / Ciphersuite is TLS-ECDH-…" — that line is the proof that the static-ECDH negotiation succeeded and your client cert was accepted.
  • If instead it stops at a handshake failure early on, you're almost certainly still on an OpenSSL-based build (see §4) or the cert/key pair isn't the one the server expects.

Once ssl_client2 completes the handshake, the hard cryptographic part is done: you have proven a mutually-authenticated TLS session to the door station, locally.

6. Registering over SIP-TLS

Now put SIP on top. PJSIP (pjproject) is the natural choice, built against the mbedTLS 3.6.x from §4 (configure pjproject to use mbedTLS, not OpenSSL — otherwise you're back behind the cipher wall).

A minimal pjsua account, with placeholders:

--id             sip:<sip-user>@<sip-domain>
--registrar      sip:<doorstation-ip>:<sip-tls-port>;transport=tls
--realm          *
--username       <sip-user>
--password       <sip-password-from-identity>     # read from your vault, never hard-code
--tls-cert-file  client.crt
--tls-privkey-file client.key

The certificate-name mismatch, and the workaround

The server's certificate doesn't present a name that matches <doorstation-ip> — the CN is a device-specific identifier (<client-cn>), not the IP you're connecting to. A strict TLS client verifies the server name and aborts on the mismatch.

The pragmatic workaround on a trusted LAN is not to pass a CA file for server verification (--tls-ca-file omitted). Without a CA to verify against, PJSIP completes the handshake instead of rejecting the name mismatch. On your own network, against your own device, that's an acceptable trade; it is not something to carry over to anything facing the internet.

Be honest about what this costs you. With no CA and no server verification, you authenticate to the door but you don't authenticate the door to you — a device on the same LAN that can intercept the connection could impersonate it. "It's only the LAN" does not erase that; it narrows who could do it. If you want the real thing, the fix is not to trust the vendor's public CA by name (the CN won't match any name you can route to), it's to pin the door's own server certificate: capture the leaf cert the door presents (e.g. save it from the ssl_client2 run in §5), trust exactly that file, and reject anything else. That gives you mutual authentication without needing a CN/SAN that matches your LAN address — pinning compares the whole certificate, so "does the name match?" stops being the question. Note the practical catch: PJSIP's built-in --tls-verify-server still does name checking, so pinning isn't a flag you flip — you verify the presented cert against your saved copy yourself (a TLS verification callback, or an out-of-band fingerprint check at connect time). Pinning is the honest endpoint here; the CA-less handshake is the quick start.

With that in place, the registrar accepts the account:

registration success, status=200 (OK)

That 200 OK is the milestone: a local, cloud-free, mutually-authenticated SIP registration to a door station the vendor intended to be reachable only through their app.

7. Handling the identity safely

The recovered material — EC private key, client certificate, SIP password — is the keys to your door station's local interface. Treat it like any other private key:

  • It is not in this repo and the .gitignore here actively blocks *.key, *.pem, *.cfg, captures and dumps from being committed.
  • Keep it in a password manager / secrets store, and have the client read it at run time rather than writing it into a config file in the clear.
  • Never paste the private key or the SIP password into an issue, a log, or a chat. If you need to share how, share this method — not your values.

8. Opening the door locally

The folklore around these devices — and an earlier revision of this very document — says the gate/lock command has to go through the vendor cloud. Reading the decompiled "slide to open" code path (§2) shows that is wrong. The open command is a SIP MESSAGE on the same local channel you already registered on. No HTTPS, no cloud relay.

How it actually works

The app's unlock action builds a tiny JSON-RPC body and sends it in a SIP MESSAGE (the SIP "instant message" / chat method) toward the door's own SIP address — the same sip:<sip-user>@<sip-domain> you register against, not the LAN IP. The body names a method and the target actuator:

// SIP MESSAGE body — method + which actuator, nothing more
{ "jsonrpc": "2.0", "id": <n>,
  "method": "<lock-set-status>",            // from the decompiled call path
  "params": { "receiver": "<actuator-addr>", // which relay: gate, lock, light…
              "status": "<on|open>" } }
  • Only the actuator address distinguishes targets. The gate, the pedestrian lock and the courtesy light are the same command with a different receiver address. Those addresses come out of the device topology (read-only), not out of guessing.
  • It is an impulse / toggle, not a stateful "locked/unlocked" — you are pulsing a relay, the same way the physical button does.

The one real trap: a single registration per account (single-AOR)

The door accepts only one active registration per SIP account. If a background service is already registered to catch rings (§9), a second client that registers to send the open command will evict the first — and now nothing is listening for the doorbell. A second REGISTER from a new contact is simply dropped.

The way through is to not open a second registration at all: send the MESSAGE through the client that is already registered. In this project the ring-catcher (§9) exposes a tiny local command channel (an MQTT topic), and the open request is handed to it; it emits the SIP MESSAGE from its already-registered endpoint (out of dialog — a REGISTER creates no dialog, and the open request isn't tied to a call). One registration, two jobs.

No real actuator address, method name, CN or SIP identity is published here — those are device-specific and live only in the owner's vault (§7). What transfers is the shape: decompile the unlock path → it's a local SIP MESSAGE with a JSON-RPC body → send it from your one registered client.

9. Catching the ring and grabbing a photo

A registration is only useful if something listens. Keep a pjsua-based client registered as a background service (one systemd unit, auto-restart) so it is always ready for the next call. When someone presses the button, the door sends an incoming SIP INVITE to the registered account — that INVITE is the "someone rang" event. Surface it however your home-automation system likes (a binary sensor flipping to ringing, an MQTT message, a notification).

The interesting part is grabbing the visitor's face without ever answering, and the two gotchas that cost the most time — both learned by measuring, not guessing.

Gotcha 1 — the media is early-media (183), never a confirmed call

The door offers video the moment it rings: it sends 183 Session Progress with media, and starts streaming before (and whether or not) you answer. So you do not send 200 OK — you let the early-media flow and record it. Point pjsua's AVI recorder at the incoming stream and you get a short clip per ring, no call ever confirmed, the physical handset still free to ring.

Record one AVI per call (rotate a fresh file on each INVITE), so a ring never overwrites the previous visitor's clip while you are still turning it into a photo.

Gotcha 2 — the AVI's declared frame rate is a lie; seek by frame index, not seconds

This is the one that will waste your afternoon. The AVI pjsua writes declares 15 fps in its header but actually encodes ~9 fps (measured: ~268 frames over ~29.7 s). So any ffmpeg -ss <seconds> seek lands on the wrong frame — ask for "2.5 s in" and you get a frame from much later, i.e. the visitor already walking out of shot.

Extract by frame index, not by time:

# WRONG: -ss trusts the lying 15 fps header and overshoots
# ffmpeg -ss 2.5 -i ring.avi -frames:v 1 out.jpg

# RIGHT: pick an absolute frame number; n starts at 0
ffmpeg -i ring.avi -vf "select='eq(n,10)'" -frames:v 1 -q:v 2 out.jpg

10 here is an example, not a magic number — it's the frame I measured as clean (~4 s after the 183, once the stream has stabilised and the visitor is centred), in daylight. The right index depends on your own ring timing and lighting (the IR illuminator at night changes it — see §10), so measure yours rather than copying 10. The method — index, not seconds — is what transfers. Verify the real rate yourself before trusting any timing:

ffprobe -v error -select_streams v:0 \
  -show_entries stream=nb_frames,avg_frame_rate,r_frame_rate,duration \
  -of default=nw=1 ring.avi

If avg_frame_rate and the header r_frame_rate disagree, believe nb_frames and index from there. A short fallback — -sseof -0.5 for the last frame — covers rings so brief the stream is cut before frame 10 exists.

Wiring it into Home Assistant over MQTT

Decode the chosen frame to JPEG in a worker thread (a small bounded queue is enough — rings are rare and short), then hand everything to Home Assistant over MQTT — no custom integration, no polling, no cloud. The glue is MQTT Discovery: the background client publishes one retained config message per entity, and HA auto-creates the devices. You publish three things, all under one MQTT device so they group neatly in HA:

  1. A binary sensor — "someone is ringing" — flipped from the incoming INVITE.
  2. A camera — the visitor photo, published as a raw JPEG on an image topic.
  3. The open buttons (§8) — gate / lock / light — each a button whose command topic the client subscribes to, so a press in HA becomes the local SIP MESSAGE.

Discovery config for the camera and one button (placeholders only — pick your own topic prefix and IDs):

// retain=true, topic: homeassistant/camera/<node>/photo/config
{ "name": "Doorbell snapshot",
  "unique_id": "<node>_photo",
  "topic": "<prefix>/<node>/photo",          // raw JPEG bytes published here on each ring
  "device": { "identifiers": ["<node>"], "name": "Door station",
              "manufacturer": "BTicino/Legrand", "model": "EasyKit" } }

// retain=true, topic: homeassistant/button/<node>/gate/config
{ "name": "Open gate",
  "unique_id": "<node>_gate",
  "command_topic": "<prefix>/<node>/open",   // client subscribes; a press -> SIP MESSAGE (§8)
  "payload_press": "gate",
  "device": { "identifiers": ["<node>"] } }

Then at runtime the client just:

  • publishes JPEG bytes to <prefix>/<node>/photo (the camera updates instantly), and
  • on a message arriving at <prefix>/<node>/open, emits the matching SIP MESSAGE from its one registered endpoint (§8) — so an HA button, an automation, or a dashboard tap opens the door locally.

Treat the open topic as what it is: a physical actuator. Two non-negotiables. The command message must be retain=false — a retained "open" would be replayed to the client every time it reconnects, opening your gate on its own after a broker or client restart. (The Discovery config above is retain=true on purpose, so entities survive a restart; the command never is.) And the broker must enforce an ACL so only HA and this client can publish/subscribe to <prefix>/<node>/open — an unauthenticated open topic is an unlocked gate on your LAN.

The whole path — ring → early-media AVI → frame by index → JPEG → MQTT camera in HA, and HA button → MQTT → local SIP MESSAGE → relay — runs on your LAN, with the cloud never in the loop, and is proven in production.

The door station as a native MQTT device in Home Assistant — opening buttons, ring sensor, on-ring snapshot (blurred here), and a log of real commands. No cloud. (The camera thumbnail is deliberately blurred; everything else is the actual device page.)

Why MQTT Discovery rather than a REST/template sensor: the client pushes on an event (a ring is rare and instantaneous), retained config survives an HA restart, and the camera takes raw JPEG on a topic with no extra plumbing. It is the lightest honest way to get a pushed image and a command path into HA.

10. Work in progress — where this is going

The hard parts are done and proven: identity extraction, the static-ECDH TLS wall, local registration, photo on ring (§9), and local door opening (§8). What remains is polish, not cryptography:

  • Full live media, not just a snapshot — negotiate the INVITE's SDP for a continuous view.
  • Richer event history — log every INVITE and its outcome for a who-rang-when record.
  • Night-time robustness — confirm the on-ring photo timing holds with the IR illuminator at night (the frame-index tuning in §9 was measured in daylight).

These land as separate, documented commits.

11. Related work

deddroid/bticino-easykit is the other serious open-source EasyKit client, and it is worth knowing about — it takes the opposite route. It reaches the kit through the Netatmo/BTicino cloud: you sign in with a Netatmo "Home + Security" account, it reuses the official app's OAuth/Firebase client IDs, and the call's video offer arrives as a Firebase push. The media is WebRTC (H.264 + Opus), not SIP. It verifies live view, panel audio, ring notifications with a snapshot, and door/gate release — all via that cloud path.

This project is the local alternative: no Netatmo account, no Firebase, no cloud in the loop. It registers directly to the door over SIP-TLS on the LAN using a client identity recovered from the app, and opens the door with a local out-of-dialog SIP MESSAGE from that registered endpoint. Where deddroid/bticino-easykit depends on the internet staying up and the cloud service staying online, this one keeps working if the WAN is cut.

One caveat worth flagging: "EasyKit" covers more than one generation of hardware. The cloud/Firebase path above looks like the Wi-Fi, Netatmo-rebadged kit; the work here targets the SIP-TLS model with its own SIP domain. If your kit behaves differently from what either README describes, you may simply have the other generation.
